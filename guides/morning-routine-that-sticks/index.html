How to build a morning routine that sticks

Nicolas DojcilovicPublished on 12 November 2026

You set the alarm early, you have a plan, and twenty minutes later you're still scrolling in bed. Morning routines rarely fail for lack of motivation: they fail because they're too big, too vague, and because the phone gets there first. Here is what research says, and how to build one that holds.

Why morning routines fall apart

Most routines start too ambitious: an hour of sport, meditation, journaling and reading, from day one. After a short night, the whole thing feels impossible, so you skip it, and the next day it's harder to start again. And in between, the phone on the nightstand offers something easier: messages, news, a feed that never ends.

A routine that holds is short, precise and protected from the phone.

How long a habit really takes

The “21 days to form a habit” you often read has no solid basis. In 2010, Phillippa Lally and her colleagues at University College London followed participants who chose a new daily habit, such as drinking a glass of water at breakfast or going for a run before dinner. It took them 66 days on average for the behaviour to become automatic, with very wide differences: from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the habit.

Two findings help: missing one day didn't significantly harm the process, and more complex behaviours took longer to become automatic. In other words: start simple, and don't give up after a missed morning.

Start small: three steps, ten minutes

Choose three steps you can do even on a bad day, and give each one a duration. For example:

  • A glass of water: 1 minute.
  • Three stretches: 3 minutes.
  • Plan the day: 2 minutes.

That's the 5-minute morning. When it's automatic, add a step: deep breathing, a cool shower, reading. The Energizing morning is a good next level. Times matter: a step with a duration ends, a vague intention never does.

Decide in advance: “when X, I do Y”

Psychologists call it an implementation intention: a plan in the form “when this happens, I do that”. In their 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that this kind of plan helped people act on their goals; a 2024 update suggests the effect is more modest than first estimated, but real.

For the morning: “when my alarm rings, I get up and drink a glass of water”, “when I've had my coffee, I start the routine”. Hook the routine to something that already happens every day.

Keep the phone out of the way

The simplest solution is physical: charge your phone outside the bedroom and use a separate alarm clock. If that's not possible, iPhone's Screen Time lets you limit apps by time slot, and some apps go further by locking the distracting apps until your routine is done. Either way, the rule is the same: the routine first, the phone after.

On the days it doesn't happen

You'll miss mornings: illness, travel, a sleepless night. According to Lally's study, a single missed day doesn't undo the habit; what breaks it is giving up after the miss. Have a minimal version for bad days (just the glass of water and one stretch) and pick up the full routine the next day.

Questions people ask

How long does it take for a morning routine to become a habit?

There's no fixed number. In Phillippa Lally's 2010 study, it took 66 days on average for a new daily behaviour to become automatic, from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the habit. Simple routines become automatic faster.

What should I do if I miss a morning?

Pick it up again the next day. Lally's study found that missing one day didn't significantly affect habit formation; giving up after a miss is what breaks it. Keep a minimal version for bad days.

How many steps should a morning routine have?

Start with three steps and about ten minutes, simple enough to do on a bad day. Add a step only when the routine feels automatic.

How do I stop checking my phone first thing in the morning?

Charge it outside the bedroom and use a separate alarm clock, or use Screen Time or an app that locks distracting apps until your routine is done. Hooking the routine to your alarm (“when it rings, I get up and drink water”) also helps.
